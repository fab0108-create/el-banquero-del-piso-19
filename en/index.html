About the book

A noir about grief, power, and the silence a whole city chooses.

Lima, August 1990. A woman lies dead on a hotel terrace with no name, no shoes, no purse—and nineteen floors above her, a single curtain hangs outward.

The police say she was a cleaning woman. Then a mother calls the newspaper: the dead woman is her daughter, Marita Alpaca, a flight attendant, and she knows who she was with that night.

Mateo Quispe, a crime reporter in a city of curfews, blackouts, and runaway inflation, is told to close the story in five lines. He can’t. What begins as a misidentified body becomes an obsession: an autopsy report that vanishes from the case file, a powerful banker who leaves the country, anonymous envelopes slipped under doors, and a headline that will echo through every street in Lima.

Based on one of Peru’s most notorious cases, The Banker on the 19th Floor is a taut, atmospheric noir about grief, power, and the silence a whole city chooses—told by the reporter who refused to look away.

A work of fiction inspired by real events. The journalist, his colleagues, and his sources are fictional characters.

Chapter 1

The Terrace

The phone woke me at ten past seven on a Sunday morning. On the crime beat, nobody calls at that hour with good news.

“Mateo. The Sheraton. A woman. She came down from up top.”

I hung up without asking anything else. Lima, August 1990: a city with the curfew lodged in its head and the blackouts lodged under its skin. The streets were empty, and I rode down the Paseo de la República in a taxi whose two yellow headlights cut through the garúa, the fine gray mist that hangs over Lima all winter. I thought I had already seen everything a city like this could show a reporter. I was wrong.

The Sheraton rose over downtown like a concrete finger. Below, on the low terrace, they had spread a white sheet. The cops were talking quietly, the way people talk when they want to make sure you don’t hear.

“No press,” one of them said.

I showed him my press card. He hesitated, looked at another cop, and the other one shrugged. It was that hour of a Sunday when the rules haven’t woken up yet.

They lifted the sheet for a second. It was enough.

A young woman, naked, her brown hair plastered to her face by the damp. Arms flung open, as if at the last second she had wanted to embrace the air. No shoes, no purse, no jewelry. Nothing that said who she was.

There’s something you learn in this trade: when someone dies without a name, it’s almost always because someone has taken the trouble to make sure she doesn’t have one.

I looked up. Nineteen floors of dark windows. Only one had its curtain blown outward, like a tongue.

“She jumped?” I asked.

The cop with the mustache took his time answering.

“That’s what the report says.”

“And what do you say?”

“I don’t say anything, sir. I just watch the sheet.”
